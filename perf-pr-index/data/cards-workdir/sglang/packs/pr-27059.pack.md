## Why the percentage improvement changed

The two result sets are not contradictory. The following decomposition shows what changed:

| Stack | FP4 E2E | Additional time observed with the FP8 indexer (`FP8 - FP4`) | FP8 E2E | E2E reduction from FP4 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Original Triton stack | 410.49 s | +30.48 s | 440.97 s | 6.91% |
| Latest FlashInfer stack | 20.53 s | +30.91 s | 51.45 s | 60.09% |

The FP8 indexer adds approximately 31 seconds in both measurements. The major change is that the FP4 path, which represents the rest of the model plus the faster indexer, decreased from 410.49 seconds to 20.53 seconds on the newer stack.

On the original stack, the approximately 30.48-second saving was small relative to the 440.97-second total, so E2E latency improved by only 6.91%. On the latest stack, the surrounding model path became much faster while the FP8-indexer overhead remained approximately 31 seconds. Removing that overhead reduced E2E latency from 51.45 seconds to 20.53 seconds, or 60.09%.

For the fixed-concurrency workload, benchmark duration decreased from approximately 51.57 seconds to 20.56 seconds. Throughput is inversely proportional to duration for a fixed amount of work, so this is a `51.57 / 20.56 = 2.508x` throughput ratio, reported as a 150.83% increase.

Therefore, the larger percentage does not mean that the FP4 indexer itself suddenly became several times faster. Its absolute E2E benefit remained approximately 31 seconds. The newer SGLang stack made the surrounding SM120 attention and model-execution path much faster, exposing the FP8 indexer as the dominant remaining bottleneck.

The full cross-run speedup should not be attributed solely to FlashInfer because the SGLang revisions also differ by roughly six weeks of development. A backend-only experiment on one fixed SGLang revision would be required to isolate the exact contribution of the attention backend.
